How to plan a mountain bike live broadcast.

Mountain bike coverage is a geography problem before it is a camera problem. The course, terrain, access, timing, connectivity, weather, and audience destinations determine the system.

Start with a course map and the story of the race

Identify the moments viewers must see: start, technical features, passing opportunities, high-speed sections, intermediate timing, finish, athlete reactions, interviews, podium, and venue activity. Then place cameras around those jobs rather than spreading them evenly just to increase camera count.

Build coverage zones

Long courses are easier to design when they are divided into visual and technical zones. Each zone should answer three questions: what can the camera see, how does its signal reach production, and how does the operator communicate with the control room?

Signal transport can dominate the design

A good camera position is not useful if its picture cannot reliably return to production. Fiber, existing venue infrastructure, point-to-point networking, wireless video, IP transport, local conversion, and hybrid approaches can all play a role. Terrain, trees, structures, distance, and access should be tested rather than assumed.

Comms matter as much as pictures

Mountain crews are often separated by large distances. Operators need reliable instructions, warning of approaching riders, and a way to report technical or safety issues. The comms design should account for terrain and dead zones before show day.

Timing and graphics give the race meaning

Viewers need more than isolated rider pictures. Start lists, names, splits, elapsed time, results, gaps, standings, and sponsor presentation make the competition understandable. Confirm early how timing data can be received and what happens if the data feed is delayed or unavailable.

Replay protects moments that cannot be repeated

Crashes, line choices, passes, saves, and finish moments often happen once. Isolated recording and replay can add enormous value, but replay cameras, record paths, operators, and storage must be included in the technical design.

Plan power and weather by camera position

Some positions may have house power; others may be battery-only for hours. Weather can change quickly at altitude. Batteries, weather protection, cable routing, grounding, heat, cold, wind, and safe operator shelter all belong in the site plan.

Remember the audience standing at the mountain

Venue screens, finish-area displays, announcers, sponsor activations, and public-address integration can make the same production useful to spectators on site. The venue feed may need different information or timing from the online program.

Design the failure plan before the show

Decide which camera losses can be worked around, which links need backup, what the director cuts to during an outage, how the stream survives an internet problem, and which records must continue even if the live path fails.
