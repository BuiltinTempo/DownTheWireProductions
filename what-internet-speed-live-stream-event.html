What internet speed do you need to livestream an event?

The encoder bitrate is only the beginning. A reliable live event needs enough upload capacity, headroom, stability, and redundancy to stay online when the venue is busy and conditions change.

Start with the actual stream bitrate

A single 1080p program feed commonly lands in the neighborhood of 5 to 10 Mbps depending on frame rate, codec, platform, motion, and quality settings. That does not mean a 10 Mbps upload connection is enough for a 10 Mbps stream. Production should have meaningful headroom above the encoded bitrate so short-term network variation does not immediately become dropped frames or a disconnected stream.

A practical rule: do not run the uplink at its limit

For a simple production, a stable upstream connection with at least two to three times the required program bitrate is a much healthier starting point than matching the encoder bitrate one-for-one. A 6 Mbps stream is far safer on a consistent 20 Mbps upstream path than on a connection that occasionally peaks at 7 or 8 Mbps.

Multi-camera does not always mean multiple internet streams

Most multi-camera productions switch camera feeds locally and send one final program feed to the internet. Camera count therefore affects the production network and signal infrastructure more than the public uplink itself. However, remote contribution feeds, cloud production, return video, remote commentators, backup encoders, ISO uploads, or multiple destinations can substantially increase bandwidth requirements.

1080p streaming bandwidth targets

For many real-world 1080p live events, it is reasonable to plan around a program bitrate in the 5–10 Mbps range and then provision significantly more stable upload capacity than that. A production with a primary 8 Mbps program feed might target 25 Mbps or more of clean, sustained upstream capacity before other production traffic is considered. Higher frame rates, higher resolutions, HEVC workflows, or multiple simultaneous outputs change the calculation.

4K needs more than a faster speed test

4K delivery can push program bitrates much higher and increases the consequences of packet loss or bandwidth swings. Before choosing 4K, make sure the audience platform, event goals, encoder, recording workflow, and venue network actually justify it. In many event environments, a stable high-quality 1080p production delivers more value than an unstable 4K stream.

Upload consistency matters more than the best number you see

A connection that measures 200 Mbps once and 8 Mbps ten minutes later is not a 200 Mbps broadcast connection. Test repeatedly, test over time, and test using the actual encoder path when possible. Look for packet loss, jitter, latency spikes, and performance changes when other venue systems are active.

Shared venue internet can change dramatically on event day

Public Wi-Fi, venue office networks, and shared business circuits may behave very differently after staff, vendors, ticketing systems, sponsors, guests, and thousands of phones arrive. Ask whether the broadcast can receive a dedicated wired port, reserved bandwidth, a production VLAN, or a separate circuit.

Cellular can be excellent—but crowd load matters

5G and LTE can provide strong event uplinks, especially when multiple carriers are bonded together. The risk is that an empty venue test does not recreate event-day tower loading. If cellular is primary, test multiple carriers and understand whether the system is actually bonding paths or simply failing over between them.

Build redundancy around the cost of failure

A free community stream and a sponsor-funded championship do not need the same network architecture. If going offline would create contractual, revenue, sponsor, ticketing, or reputational consequences, plan a genuinely separate backup path. Ideally the backup should not depend on the same ISP, building switch, fiber handoff, power source, or cellular carrier as the primary.

Do not forget the rest of the production network

Streaming bandwidth is only one piece of the system. Camera control, comms, graphics, timing, replay, NDI or SMPTE ST 2110 workflows, remote contribution, monitoring, and file transfer may all compete for network resources. Keep production-critical traffic separated and documented where practical.

Test the exact destination before show day

Run a sustained test to the real streaming platform or CDN. Watch encoder health, dropped frames, reconnect behavior, latency, and bitrate stability. Then deliberately fail the primary connection and verify how the backup behaves. A redundancy plan is not complete until the crew knows what actually happens during a failure.
